## Post 1: The Anatomy of a Pavé Setting

**Handle:** anatomy-of-pave-setting
**Author:** Pavé Studio
**Tags:** craft, education, settings
**Published:** yes

### Body

The term *pavé* — from the French for "paved" — describes a setting technique where small stones are set so close together they appear to pave the surface of the metal entirely in diamond. But the simplicity of the name belies the difficulty of the work.

Each stone in a pavé row sits in a hand-drilled seat, held in place by tiny beads of metal raised from the surface around it. The setter works under magnification, shaping and burnishing each bead individually. A single bracelet might hold three hundred stones. Three hundred seats. Twelve hundred beads. All by hand.

**Why it matters**

A well-executed pavé setting catches light from every angle — because the stones are set flush to the metal, they form a continuous reflective surface. A poorly executed one chips and loses stones within months, because the beads weren't properly worked.

We test every piece at three points during the setting process: before setting, after setting, and after the final polish. The polish vibrates the piece; any loose stone will show itself then.

**The tools**

A pavé setter's bench holds dozens of gravers — small cutting tools in different profiles — along with bead raisers, burnishers, and a ball burr for drilling the stone seats. Most setters grind their own gravers from raw steel blanks. The angle of the edge, the width of the face: these details are personal.

## Post 2: How to Choose a Diamond — A Field Guide

**Handle:** how-to-choose-a-diamond
**Author:** Pavé Studio
**Tags:** education, diamonds, buying guide
**Published:** yes

### Body

The four Cs — cut, colour, clarity, carat — were codified by the GIA in the 1950s to give buyers a common language. They are genuinely useful, but they are not the whole story.

**Cut is everything**

If you can only care about one thing, care about cut. Cut determines how well the stone interacts with light — its brightness (how much white light it returns), fire (how it splits light into colour), and scintillation (how it sparkles when it moves). A well-cut stone in the G-H colour range and VS2 clarity will outperform a D-IF stone with a mediocre cut every time, for a fraction of the price.

For round brilliants, aim for Excellent or Ideal cut grades. For fancy shapes (cushion, oval, pear, emerald), cut grading is more subjective — look at the stone in person, or at least in multiple lighting conditions.

**Colour: the grey area**

The GIA grades colour from D (colourless) to Z (light yellow). The visible difference between D and G is imperceptible to most eyes in a face-up stone. We recommend G–H for white gold settings, and I–J for yellow or rose gold settings, where any slight warmth in the stone is masked by the metal.

**Clarity: invisible inclusions**

Most inclusions are invisible to the naked eye. A stone graded VS2 (very slightly included) will appear perfectly clean under casual viewing. For larger stones or emerald cuts (which have a more open, reflective table), consider VS1 or better.

**Carat: size versus weight**

Carat is a measure of weight, not size. Two 1-carat diamonds with different cuts can look dramatically different in diameter. A well-cut stone will maximise its spread; a deep-cut stone carries weight in the pavilion, where you can't see it.

## Post 3: On Caring for Fine Jewelry

**Handle:** caring-for-fine-jewelry
**Author:** Pavé Studio
**Tags:** care, education, tips
**Published:** yes

### Body

Fine jewelry is made to last generations. With some basic attention, your pieces will look exactly as they did on the day you first wore them — even after decades of daily wear.

**What to avoid**

Chlorine is the enemy of gold. It reacts with the alloy metals — most notably copper — and can cause structural weakening over time that isn't visible until a prong breaks. Remove your rings before swimming, hot-tubbing, or cleaning with bleach-based products.

Ultrasonic cleaners are excellent for plain gold and diamonds, but destructive for some coloured stones (particularly emeralds, opals, and tanzanite) and for any piece with an adhesive element. When in doubt, ask.

Perfume and hand cream should be applied before putting on jewelry, not after. The alcohols and oils in these products can cloud stones and dull gold over time.

**Daily care**

A soft, lint-free cloth — the kind that comes in every Pavé box — removes skin oils, which are the primary cause of dullness. A quick wipe before storing is the single most effective care habit.

Store pieces individually, or at least with soft pouches between them. Diamond is the hardest natural material; it will scratch gold, and it will scratch other diamonds if pieces are jumbled together.

**Professional cleaning**

We recommend an annual professional clean and inspection for any piece you wear regularly. The inspection is as important as the clean — it catches loose prongs or worn settings before they become lost stones.

## Post 4: The Shape Question — Choosing a Stone Shape

**Handle:** choosing-stone-shape
**Author:** Pavé Studio
**Tags:** education, diamonds, engagement rings
**Published:** yes

### Body

The shape of a stone does more than any other single factor to determine the personality of a ring. Here's how we think about the main options.

**Round brilliant**

The classic. A round brilliant has 57 or 58 facets optimised over more than a century of refinement to maximise light return. It is the brightest shape, the most versatile, and the most forgiving of colour and clarity grades. If you're unsure, start here.

**Cushion**

The cushion cut is a square or rectangular shape with rounded corners and larger facets that produce a softer, more romantic light pattern. It dates to the 18th century — what was then called an "old mine cut" — and has been refined into various modern profiles. Some cushions have a crushed-ice appearance; others (particularly "antique cushions") show larger, more distinct facets. Personal preference dictates which you want.

**Oval**

An oval brilliant has the same facet structure as a round brilliant, but its elongated shape creates the appearance of a larger stone and lengthens the finger. The proportions — length-to-width ratio, depth percentage, and where the bow-tie shadow falls — vary considerably. We prefer ratios between 1.35:1 and 1.50:1 for most hands.

**Pear**

A pear combines a round half with a pointed tip, producing a graceful teardrop silhouette. It requires more precise setting to protect the tip and must be evaluated carefully for symmetry — the axis from tip to cleft should be perfectly straight.

**Emerald**

An emerald cut (named for the step-cut faceting traditionally used for emerald gemstones) is a rectangle with cut corners and long, parallel facets that produce a "hall of mirrors" reflection rather than sparkle. It is the most architectural shape, reads as contemporary or vintage depending on context, and requires higher clarity grades than other shapes because its open table makes inclusions more visible.
